# Research Angle 1: Anatomy of an Excellent Consulting Deliverable

## Key Findings

### Finding 1: The "Pyramid Principle" — Answer First, Evidence Second
Barbara Minto developed the Pyramid Principle at McKinsey in the 1960s; it remains the single most cited structural standard for consulting deliverables. The core rule is: state the recommendation first, then provide supporting evidence — not the other way around. Every slide should carry an "action title" (a complete sentence stating the conclusion, not just a topic), so a reader who only reads the titles still understands the entire argument. MBB firms enforce this because partners and C-suite clients have no patience to read to the end before knowing where the argument lands.

**Source:** "The Minto Pyramid Principle: How McKinsey Structures Slides" — https://winningpresentations.com/pyramid-principle-presentations/ ; "Consulting Slide Standards: Rules McKinsey, BCG & Bain Follow" — https://deckary.com/blog/consulting-slide-standards ; "The Pyramid Principle - Consulting Toolbox" — https://slideworks.io/resources/the-pyramid-principle-mckinsey-toolbox-with-examples

### Finding 2: One Message Per Slide — The "So What" Test
The universal quality gate at MBB firms is whether each slide passes the "So What" test: every element on the slide must answer the question "Why should I believe this action title?" If content doesn't support the title's single point, it gets cut. The rule is: if you find yourself writing "and" in your action title, you have two slides, not one. This is not a design preference — it is a structural discipline that eliminates cognitive overload. Slides that carry multiple messages force the reader to prioritise, which is the analyst's job, not the client's.

**Source:** "How McKinsey Consultants Make Presentations" — https://slideworks.io/resources/how-mckinsey-consultants-make-presentations ; "McKinsey Presentation Structure" — https://slidemodel.com/mckinsey-presentation-structure/ ; "Consulting Presentations: MBB Slide Design & Structure Guide" — https://deckary.com/blog/pillar-consulting-presentations-guide

### Finding 3: Evidence Quality — Primary vs. Secondary Research as a Differentiator
The most significant divide between a strong and a weak consulting deliverable is the quality of the underlying evidence. Secondary research (existing reports, databases, articles) is the baseline — any analyst can do it. What distinguishes top-tier deliverables is original primary research: customer interviews, expert calls, surveys, proprietary data collection. Primary research gives insights that the client literally could not have found themselves, and it is the foundation of the "Aha!" moment clients pay for. One UCLA 180DC client testimonial cited the team's sourcing of original references and explicit documentation of assumptions as reasons the deliverable was excellent; this is rare even at the student-consulting level.

**Source:** "Primary Research vs Secondary Research for 2026" — https://research.com/research/primary-research-vs-secondary-research ; "180 Degrees Consulting UCLA" — https://www.180degreesusc.org/ (client testimonials section); "How accurate is secondary market research vs primary research?" — https://aexus.com/how-accurate-is-secondary-market-research-vs-primary-research/

### Finding 4: The ERIP Model — Four Academic Dimensions of Consulting Service Quality
A peer-reviewed 2020 study published in the Journal of Business & Industrial Marketing (Veres, Varga-Toldi) derived the ERIP model from 22 expert interviews in the financial sector, covering both clients and consultants. Through grounded theory analysis of a 600-page interview transcript, clients were found to evaluate consulting quality on five attributes (consultants, client adaptation, consulting process, deliverables, communication), which collapsed into four separable dimensions: **Expertise** (technical knowledge and analytical rigour), **Relations** (trust, communication, client-fit), **Involvement** (client co-creation and buy-in), and **Performance** (whether the deliverable actually changes outcomes). Deliverables alone — the physical output — were only one of the five raw attributes; the other four are interpersonal and process dimensions. This means excellent deliverables are necessary but not sufficient for client satisfaction.

**Source:** "ERIP: service quality model of management consulting projects" — https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/JBIM-05-2020-0226/full/html ; ResearchGate abstract — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/347063018_ERIP_service_quality_model_of_management_consulting_projects

### Finding 5: The 2025 AI Consulting Benchmark — Five Rubric Criteria for Consulting Deliverable Quality
A 2025 arXiv paper ("Evaluating Deep Research Agents on Expert Consulting Work," arXiv:2605.17554) built a benchmark of 42 expert-authored consulting prompts to evaluate AI agents on realistic consulting work. The SME rubric developed by practitioners with 15+ years of consulting experience — specifically designed to evaluate management consulting deliverables and validated to be MECE — uses five criteria: **Data Integrity** (accuracy and sourcing of data), **Analytical Rigor** (soundness of reasoning, avoidance of logical leaps), **Relevance & Focus** (degree to which output addresses the actual client problem), **Execution Precision** (completeness, no gaps, follows the brief), and **Format & Deliverability** (usability and presentation quality of the output).

**Source:** "Evaluating Deep Research Agents on Expert Consulting Work: A Benchmark with Verifiers, Rubrics, and Cognitive Traps" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17554 ; HTML version — https://arxiv.org/html/2605.17554

### Finding 6: What Clients Actually Value — CMCE Research on Consultant Value Add
The Centre for Management Consulting Excellence (CMCE) UK research report found strong agreement between clients and consultants that the most valuable consulting contribution is **skills and knowledge transfer** — leaving the client better equipped after the engagement ends, not just delivering a document. There is a structural gap: "clients do not always engage consultants to provide the services they consider most valuable." 57% of clients said coaching client staff to perform project activities was the best way to deliver value — but none of these clients considered this factor when selecting consultants. This implies that deliverables designed to be reusable, self-teaching, and transparent about methodology score substantially higher on long-term client satisfaction.

**Source:** "Research report: Consultant Value Add: Maximising Value from your Management Consultant" — https://www.cmce.org.uk/article/research-report-consultant-value-add-maximising-value-your-management-consultant ; "CMCE Management Consultant Value Report" — https://www.cmce.org.uk/knowledge-bank/cmce-management-consultant-value-report

### Finding 7: The "Aha + I Knew That" Balance — Insight Calibration
Practitioner authority David A. Fields articulates a quality dimension often left implicit: the best deliverable combines two types of insight in calibrated proportions. Some findings should confirm what the client already suspected but couldn't prove ("I knew that") — this validates the client's judgment and makes them feel smart. Other findings must be genuinely surprising and new ("Aha!") — this justifies the consulting fee. A deliverable that is only confirmatory makes the client wonder why they hired you. One that is entirely novel makes them feel disoriented and skeptical. The balance is the mark of a senior consultant who understood the client's prior beliefs before writing a single slide.

**Source:** "What Your Consulting Effort is Worth to Clients" — https://www.davidafields.com/what-your-consulting-effort-is-worth-to-clients-a-surprising-analysis/ ; "The Perfect Consulting Deliverable" — https://davidafields.com/the-perfect-consulting-deliverable/

### Finding 8: Generic Recommendations Are the Most Common Fatal Flaw
Multiple practitioners identify the same root cause of poor consulting deliverables: recommendations that could apply to any company in any sector. Weak reports list tasks completed rather than connecting actions to business outcomes. Strong reports have recommendations that: (a) name specific actions, (b) quantify the impact, (c) assign an owner, and (d) specify a timeframe. The practitioner standard is 3–7 concrete actions, each with a mini-business case: what it solves, what it costs, what success looks like. Rule of thumb: "A 40-page report full of charts is not a deliverable — it's a data dump. The consultant's job is to tell the client what the data means and what to do about it."

**Source:** "The 7 essential rules of writing a good consultancy report" — https://www.flyingsolo.com.au/startup/the-7-essential-rules-of-writing-a-good-consultancy-report/ ; "Better consulting reports in 10 steps" — https://www.writing-skills.com/knowledge-hub/better-consulting-reports-in-10-steps/ ; "Consulting Report Template" — https://www.consultingsuccess.com/consulting-report-template

### Finding 9: The SCR Narrative — Situation, Complication, Resolution
McKinsey's SCR (Situation-Complication-Resolution) framework is the narrative spine that separates a coherent consulting deck from a slide dump. The Situation establishes neutral current-state context. The Complication introduces the specific problem or gap creating urgency. The Resolution presents the recommended response. The Resolution section should account for the majority of slides — clients are more interested in solutions than context. Decks that get stuck in the Situation phase (lots of market data, lots of "as-is" analysis) before reaching the Resolution are structurally weak regardless of how good the individual analysis is. Senior consultants spend disproportionate time on the Complication — articulating exactly why the status quo is untenable — because this is what makes the Resolution feel necessary rather than optional.

**Source:** "How to use the SCR framework" — https://slideworks.io/resources/how-to-use-McKinseys-scr-framework-with-examples ; "McKinsey SCR Framework" — https://managementconsulted.com/mckinsey-scr-framework/ ; "Situation Complication Resolution SCR Framework" — https://www.stratechi.com/situation-complication-resolution-scr-framework/

### Finding 10: AI Quality Uplift — The BCG/HBS "Jagged Frontier" Study
A 2023 HBS/BCG field experiment with 758 BCG consultants found that AI-assisted consultants completed 12.2% more tasks, worked 25.1% faster, and produced work rated 32% higher in quality by human graders. The strongest effect was on the lower half of performers: they saw a 43% quality improvement — meaning AI compresses the quality distribution upward, pulling weaker output toward the professional standard. The study also found a "jagged frontier": AI excels at within-frontier tasks (analysis, synthesis, writing) but actively degrades quality on outside-frontier tasks requiring real-world judgment or reconciling contradictory evidence.

**Source:** "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier" — https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700 ; Harvard Crimson — https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2023/10/13/jagged-edge-ai-bcg/ ; VentureBeat — https://venturebeat.com/ai/enterprise-workers-gain-40-percent-performance-boost-from-gpt-4-harvard-study-finds/

## Quality Dimensions Framework

Synthesised from arXiv:2605.17554 (the only MECE academic rubric purpose-built for consulting deliverables), ERIP (the most rigorous academic service-quality model for consulting), and MBB practitioner standards:

**Dimension 1: Analytical Rigour**
Does the reasoning hold up under scrutiny? Are data sources cited and correctly interpreted? Are assumptions made explicit rather than hidden? Is quantitative analysis present where qualitative claims could be quantified?
*Weak signal:* claims unsupported by data; conclusions that exceed what the evidence supports.

**Dimension 2: Evidence Quality & Sourcing**
Is primary research present (interviews, surveys, proprietary data)? Are secondary sources high-quality (annual reports, academic papers, official statistics)? Are sources cited explicitly, allowing the client to verify?
*Weak signal:* no citations; all data from generic industry reports; no evidence the consultant spoke with domain experts.

**Dimension 3: Insight Specificity & Actionability**
Are recommendations tailored to this specific client's situation? Does each recommendation have an owner, timeframe, and defined success metric? Is there a quantified impact or cost estimate?
*Weak signal:* generic recommendations applicable to any company; action items without owners; no prioritisation.

**Dimension 4: Narrative Structure & Logical Flow**
Does the deliverable follow a coherent SCR arc? Can an executive reading only the action titles understand the full argument? Does each section build on the previous one?
*Weak signal:* analysis dump without a story; no clear "so what"; data presented without connecting to recommendations.

**Dimension 5: Relevance & Focus**
Does the deliverable directly address the stated problem? Are the most important findings front-loaded, not buried? Is scope creep avoided?
*Weak signal:* large appendices containing the real analysis; weak executive summary; deliverable answers a slightly different question than the one asked.

**Dimension 6: Transparency of Methodology**
Is the scoring or evaluation methodology explained? Are the criteria for comparing options defined before the comparison? Are limitations acknowledged?
*Weak signal:* conclusions presented without showing the work; opaque scoring; no discussion of what was out of scope.

**Dimension 7: Format & Deliverability**
Is the deliverable standalone — does it communicate fully without the presenter? Is it appropriately concise (practitioner standard: under 20 pages for main body)? Is it visually consistent and professionally formatted?
*Weak signal:* requires extensive verbal walkthrough to make sense; cluttered slides; inconsistent formatting.

**Dimension 8: Client-Contextual Fit**
Does the deliverable reflect genuine understanding of the client's organisation, constraints, and political context? Does it balance "Aha!" insights with confirming the client's existing correct instincts? Are recommendations feasible given the client's actual resources?
*Weak signal:* technically correct but operationally or politically impossible recommendations; no evidence of client context in the analysis.

## Implications for 180DC / AI Improvement

- **Evidence quality is the largest addressable gap.** Most student teams rely entirely on secondary sources because primary research (interviews, expert calls) is time-consuming to organise. AI cannot conduct interviews — but it can dramatically accelerate secondary research quality by synthesising annual reports, academic literature, and market data into structured, cited summaries. This raises the baseline evidence quality for every team, every project.

- **Methodology transparency is achievable at near-zero marginal cost with AI.** Explicitly documented scoring criteria, assumption logs, and source attribution are exactly the type of structured writing that AI excels at generating consistently. A well-prompted Claude can produce a transparent methodology section in minutes that would take a student team hours to draft from scratch — directly replicating the quality marker observed in the excellent Verde Foods deliverable.

- **Generic recommendations are the most common failure mode and are directly fixable.** AI can pressure-test recommendations for specificity: "Is this recommendation specific to this company, or could it apply to any company in this sector?" This single quality gate, applied systematically, would eliminate the most frequent client complaint about student consulting deliverables.

- **Narrative structure (SCR) can be enforced at the template level.** The gap between a coherent deck and a slide dump is often structural, not analytical — teams have the insights but don't know how to arrange them. AI-assisted outlines enforcing a Situation-Complication-Resolution arc, with action titles pre-populated from the analysis, would raise structural quality across all 180DC deliverables without requiring individual consultant training.

- **The BCG/HBS finding that AI compresses the quality distribution upward is directly applicable to 180DC.** The weakest-performing teams (those without top-tier mentors, at less-resourced branches, or with limited analytical experience) benefit most from AI assistance — exactly where the quality gap is largest. A Claude-assisted research-and-drafting workflow could bring bottom-quartile 180DC deliverables closer to the standard that the best teams currently produce.
